YOUR JOB — the director's cut plus the story map. Splitting is your work;
nobody downstream re-cuts your scenes. Work through the whole narration from
the first word to the last.

1. SCENES. Every scene is ONE complete visual thought or action: a subject, a
   place, an object, a reveal, a transition. Start a new scene when the subject,
   action, location, reveal or visual purpose changes. Do NOT write what is on
   screen — a later stage does that with the story map in hand. For every scene
   return only: word_start_index, word_end_index, scene_role
   (event|portrait|location|object|abstract|transition), chapter_id.

   SCENE LENGTH — a hard rule, checked scene by scene:
   - regular scenes: from {{MIN_DURATION}} to {{MAX_DURATION}} seconds of
     narration (measure with the word start/end times);
   - do not make a scene shorter than {{MIN_DURATION}} s — the viewer has no
     time to read the frame;
   - do not make a scene longer than {{MAX_DURATION}} s — the viewer gets
     bored; when a thought needs more, split it into two scenes with different
     angles;
   - the opening block, first {{INTRO_BOUNDARY}} s of narration: every scene
     strictly {{INTRO_MIN}}-{{INTRO_MAX}} s — a faster, hook-driven cut that
     overrides the semantic pacing for this block.
   Pacing inside the range is yours: shorter scenes for speed, surprise and
   accents; longer ones for immersion and reflection. Alternate them.
   Cover every word exactly once, in order, without gaps or overlap.

2. CHAPTERS. Group consecutive scenes into chapters — the natural parts of the
   story (an act, a location, a period, a turn). Each chapter: id
   ("chapter_1", "chapter_2", …) and summary — one sentence of what happens in
   it and who is on stage. Every scene names its chapter_id; chapters follow
   each other without gaps.
   The summary is the visual context of the whole chapter: it MUST name the
   period and the place this chapter happens in, because every search query
   and every frame check of the chapter is grounded in it. Chapters of one
   narration may sit in different times and countries — a chapter about today
   says "today", not the period of the opening. Never write a bare label like
   "Chapter 2" or "continuation".

3. ENTITIES. The cast of the whole narration: every named or clearly
   identifiable person, place, object, organisation, event or abstract subject
   that the visuals will need. Each entity: id (stable snake_case identifier
   reused everywhere, e.g. "anna_german", "concert_hall_moscow"), type
   (person|location|object|organisation|event|abstract), label (short
   human-readable name), first_appearance_word_index (zero-based index of the
   word where it first appears, or null when unclear). Name only what the
   narration supports; never invent.
   When the narration never gives a name, the label MUST still describe the
   subject from what the narration DOES say — nationality or place, occupation
   or role, period: "Polish female pop singer, mid-1980s", "state music export
   agency of communist Poland". A label like "Unnamed female artist",
   "Unknown singer" or "the woman" is FORBIDDEN: this label is what later
   becomes the search query and the brief for checking the found frames, and a
   nameless label makes them match anyone in the world. Keep the id stable and
   short even for such entities ("artystka", "agencja_autorska").
   The COUNTRY is usually knowable even when its name is never spoken, and it
   belongs in the labels and the theme. Work it out from the evidence TAKEN
   TOGETHER: the institutions named (an authors' rights agency, a state
   broadcaster, a currency, a ministry, a state record label), the borders and
   neighbours ("beyond the eastern border" is a neighbour of the country the
   story is set in), the cities, the period, and the names of people.
   The language of the narration is only a WEAK hint and NEVER decides on its
   own: a story told in Spanish may be about Morocco or Brazil, one told in
   Russian may be about Kazakhstan. Language counts for something only when
   the narrator speaks of that country as his own — "here", "at home", "in our
   country" — and nothing in the story points elsewhere; then the narrator's
   language and his country agree. Whenever the content names or implies a
   DIFFERENT country than the language would suggest, the content wins.
   Write the country you concluded into the labels — "Polish female singer,
   mid-1980s", not "Eastern European singer"; "the artist's Polish home city",
   not "her home city". A region instead of a country makes every later search
   match the wrong nation: a scene about a Polish singer of the 1980s was
   filled with an American star because the label said only "female artist".
   When the evidence genuinely points at no single country — or contradicts
   itself — stay with the wider region and say so in the theme, rather than
   guessing a country.

4. THEME. One sentence: genre, main subject and setting/period of the whole
   narration — the background every search query will be grounded in.
